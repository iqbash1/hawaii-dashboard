Homelessness is more about housing costs than income.

Many residents believe homelessness here is a story about poverty, addiction and untreated illness. Honolulu’s mayor wrote about it this month. The fifty-state numbers tell a simpler story: where homes cost more, more people are homeless.

Adjusting for cost of living, Alabama’s income per person is about the same as Hawaiʻi’s. Yet in the 2024 count, Alabama had about 5 people sleeping unsheltered per 10,000 residents, compared with about 28 in Hawaiʻi.

Poverty doesn’t really explain the pattern. Mississippi, the poorest state in the country, has one of the lowest street-homelessness rates. West Virginia, where homes are the cheapest in the nation and the opioid crisis hit hardest, runs about the same rate as Alabama.

What predicts a state’s street homelessness is the price of a home. Measure prices against local incomes and rank the states: the five priciest hold five of the eight worst spots. Among the five cheapest, street homelessness never reaches a fifth of Hawaiʻi’s rate.

Researchers who tested the usual explanations, poverty, drugs, mental illness, weather, found those factors predict who becomes homeless, not how many people do. Housing markets set the how-many. A federal study found a $100 rent increase comes with about 9 percent more homelessness, after accounting for wages and poverty.

The pattern is not about warm winters, and it survives counting everyone: in HUD’s fuller 2025 tally of sheltered and unsheltered together, the ten priciest housing markets all sit above the median state, and cold New York and Vermont rank among the nation’s worst. Winters change where people sleep, not how many are homeless.

In the 2024 count, Hawaiʻi’s unsheltered rate was third-worst in the nation, behind Oregon and California.

Personal catastrophe picks who ends up on the street. Home prices set how many. Hawaiʻi’s are the highest in the country.
